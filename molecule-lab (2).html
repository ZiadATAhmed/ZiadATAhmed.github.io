An element is a promise about protons

There are only about 90 kinds of atom that occur naturally, and every single thing you have ever touched is built out of them. What separates one kind from another is not size or color or how it smells. It is the number of protons in the nucleus. Six protons is carbon. Always. Add a seventh and it stops being carbon and becomes nitrogen instead.

That is what an element is: a substance made of only one kind of atom. Neutrons can vary, and you get isotopes of the same element. Electrons can vary, and you get ions of the same element. The proton count is the part that cannot move.

The table is arranged by behavior, not by size

The periodic table looks like a filing cabinet, and students usually treat it that way. It is not. It is sorted so that atoms which behave the same way end up in the same column, and the reason is sitting in the outer shell.

Every element in a column has the same number of outer electrons. Same number of outer electrons means the same gap to fill, which means the same bonding behavior. Sodium and lithium both hand over one electron. Fluorine and chlorine both grab one. That is why the column, called a group, is the most useful thing on the table.

Groups run down. Periods run across, and a period number tells you how many shells the atom is using. Row 1 has one shell, row 2 has two, row 3 has three.

Tap a column number or any element to see what that group does.

Metals on the left, nonmetals on the right

There is a second split running through the table, and it decides which kind of bond you get. Metals sit on the left and give electrons away. Nonmetals sit on the right and take or share them. A metal meeting a nonmetal gives you an ionic bond. Two nonmetals give you a covalent bond. Two metals give you the shared electron pool that makes a wire bend instead of snap.

So you can predict the bond before you build anything. Look up both elements, check which side of the table each one sits on, and you already know the answer.

Why atoms bond at all

Atoms are not trying to be friendly. A bond forms for one reason: the atoms involved end up in a lower energy arrangement together than they were apart. In middle school terms, every atom is chasing a full outer shell, because a full outer shell is the settled, low energy state.

The first shell holds 2 electrons. The next two hold 8 each. Helium and neon already have a full outer shell, which is why they sit in the corner of the room and react with nothing. Every other atom on this page has a gap, and that gap is the whole reason chemistry happens.

Roll the ball toward the dip. Bonded is downhill, and that is why the bond holds.

Three ways to fill a shell

An atom with a gap has options. Which one it takes depends almost entirely on whether it is a metal.

Ionic: hand it over

A metal like sodium has one lonely outer electron sitting above a shell that is already full. Giving it away is cheaper than collecting seven more. A nonmetal like chlorine is one short, so it takes it. Now one particle is positive, one is negative, and the pull between opposite charges is the bond. Those charged particles are called ions.

Covalent: share it

Two nonmetals cannot do that. Neither one will give an electron up, so instead they overlap and share a pair. Here is the part students miss: a shared pair is counted by both atoms. Nobody loses anything and both shells read as fuller.

Metallic: pool it

Two metals together do something stranger. They all release their outer electrons into a shared pool that drifts through the whole lump. That is why metals conduct electricity, why they bend instead of shattering, and why a copper wire is not made of molecules.

One bond, two bonds, three bonds

Atoms can share more than one pair. Oxygen is two electrons short, so two oxygen atoms share two pairs and make a double bond. Nitrogen is three short, so nitrogen gas has a triple bond. More shared pairs pulls the atoms closer and makes the bond harder to break.

That triple bond in the air around you is one of the strongest in chemistry, which is why plants cannot use nitrogen straight from the atmosphere.

Sharing is rarely equal

Two identical atoms share a pair right down the middle. Two different atoms almost never do. Oxygen pulls harder on electrons than hydrogen does, so in water the shared pairs sit closer to the oxygen. The oxygen end goes slightly negative and the hydrogen ends go slightly positive.

That small lopsidedness is why water sticks to itself, climbs up a plant stem, dissolves salt, and beads on a leaf. Ionic and covalent are not really two boxes. They are two ends of a slider, and polar covalent lives in between.

Compound and molecule are not the same word

Students mix these two up more than any other pair in this unit, and it is not carelessness. It is that the two words answer different questions, and nobody says so out loud.

  • Compound answers what is in it. Two or more different elements chemically bonded together, in a fixed ratio.
  • Molecule answers how it is held together. A set of atoms bonded into a separate unit that you could, in principle, pick up on its own.

Because they answer different questions, all four combinations exist. That is the whole difficulty, and the grid below is the fastest way to see it.

Oxygen gas is a molecule but not a compound, because both atoms are the same element. Table salt is a compound but not a molecule, because there is no unit of two atoms in there to point at. Water is both. Copper is neither.

A quick test. Ask whether the atoms are different kinds, and you have answered element or compound. Ask whether you could lift out one repeating unit, and you have answered molecule or not. Answer both questions every time and the confusion goes away.

Where mixtures come in

A compound is bonded and fixed. A mixture is neither. Air is not a compound, because the nitrogen and oxygen are not bonded to each other and the ratio changes from one room to another. Boil salt water and the salt is still salt, because nothing was ever bonded in the first place.

Reading a formula

Change any subscript and it is a different substance with different properties, which is why H₂O and H₂O₂ behave nothing alike.
